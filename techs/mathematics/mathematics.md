# Mathematics

**Invented and reinvented across the world for over 5,000 years. Big jumps: Egyptian and Babylonian (~2000 BCE), Greek (~500 BCE), Indian (~500 CE — including zero), Islamic (~800 CE — algebra), European (~1600 CE onward).**

Math is the closest thing humans have ever built to a universal language. If you meet an alien tomorrow, "2 + 2 = 4" would probably be the first thing you could agree on. Every civilization that got big enough figured out some form of math, and they all figured out the same things — because math is discovered, not invented.

But **who** figured out **what** and **when** matters a lot for what school actually teaches you. The version of math history in most textbooks skips huge chapters. Here is the real map.

## The Egyptians and Babylonians (~2000 BCE)

The first serious mathematics we have written down comes from **Egypt and Mesopotamia**, roughly 4,000 years ago. Egyptians could do fractions, solve for unknowns, calculate the area of triangles and circles (their value for pi was a rough 3.16, close to the real 3.14159...). They needed all this to build pyramids and manage the flooding of the Nile.

The Babylonians had an even more sophisticated math. They used a **base-60 number system** — which is why we still divide hours into 60 minutes, minutes into 60 seconds, and a circle into 360 degrees. The Babylonians did this **4,000 years ago** and we are still using it because it turned out to be a very smart choice (60 divides evenly by 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, and 60 — way more than 10).

## The Greeks (~500 BCE)

The Greeks did something new: they turned math into **proofs**. Before Greek mathematics, if you noticed that a2 + b2 = c2 for a right triangle, you might just remember it. The Greeks — starting with Pythagoras and formalized by **Euclid** around 300 BCE — asked: **why is this always true?** They developed the idea that you should prove things from a small number of starting axioms.

**Euclid's *Elements*** is one of the most influential books ever written. It stayed the standard geometry textbook for **2,000 years**. When you learn geometry in high school, you are essentially learning from Euclid.

Other Greek math superstars: **Archimedes** (worked out how to calculate the area under curves, essentially inventing early calculus; also worked out levers, buoyancy, and pi to a much more accurate value). **Eratosthenes** (measured the circumference of the Earth in 240 BCE, using shadows, to within 2% of the modern value).

The Greeks also had a big blind spot: they didn't really have a concept of **zero** as a number. This turned out to be a huge problem, and would be solved somewhere else.

## India (~500 CE — the invention of zero)

**Zero** is probably the single most important mathematical invention nobody thinks about.

For most of human history, math had a big problem: how do you write "no apples"? You could write "5 apples" or "10 apples" but writing "zero apples" as a number that you can do math with — that took thousands of years to figure out.

The Chinese, the Maya, and the Babylonians all had **placeholder zeros** (a way to say "there's nothing in this column"). But turning zero into a **real number you can add, subtract, multiply, and divide with** — that was Indian mathematicians, sometime around **500 CE**.

The Indian mathematician **Brahmagupta** (628 CE) wrote the first known rules for arithmetic with zero and negative numbers. Try to imagine doing math without those. You can't.

Indian mathematicians also invented the number system we still use — **1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 0** — which is much better than Roman numerals (I, V, X, L, C, D, M) because you can actually do multiplication and division with it.

## The Islamic Golden Age (~800 CE — algebra)

Muslim mathematicians took the Indian number system, translated it into Arabic, and passed it westward to Europe. That is why we still call them **Arabic numerals** — Europe learned them from Arabic books.

Around **820 CE**, an Uzbek mathematician working in Baghdad named **Al-Khwarizmi** wrote a book with a long Arabic title. The short version was *al-jabr* — meaning "reunion of broken parts." Al-jabr became **algebra**.

Algebra is the branch of math where you use letters (x, y, z) to stand for unknown numbers, and figure out equations. Every equation you have ever solved starts here.

Al-Khwarizmi's name, translated into Latin as *Algoritmi*, gave us the word **algorithm**. Every time you say the word "algorithm" — about Google, about TikTok, about anything a computer does — you are naming a 9th-century Muslim mathematician.

See our [Arabia page](../../civs/arabia/) for the full story.

## Then Europe caught up (~1600 CE)

By the time Europe emerged from its Middle Ages, it had inherited Greek geometry, Indian numerals, and Islamic algebra. Then it started building on all of them.

- **Descartes** invented analytical geometry — combining algebra and geometry so you could describe a shape with an equation (that's where the "x-y plane" comes from).
- **Newton and Leibniz** (independently, in the 1660s and 1670s) invented **calculus** — the math of change and motion — which is what makes all modern physics possible.
- **Euler**, in the 1700s, wrote so much math that people still argue about how much of modern math is actually his.

Every one of these was building on 4,000 years of math done everywhere else in the world.

## What humanity kept

Math is the closest thing we have to a shared human language. It works the same in every country. It travels across languages and religions and centuries better than anything else humans make.

Every piece of technology you use — every phone, every car, every plane, every computer, every video game, every microwave — runs on math. Every scientific fact we know is expressed in math. Every piece of engineering that stands up runs on math.

And it is a truly global inheritance. If you learn algebra in school, you are studying an 9th-century Muslim mathematician. If you use zero, you are using an Indian invention. If you write a number, you are writing an adapted Indian symbol. If you do geometry, you are using Greek rules. If you tell time, you are counting in Babylonian base-60.

**Nobody owns math. Everyone helped build it.**
