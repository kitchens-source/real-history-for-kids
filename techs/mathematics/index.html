Mathematics

Invented and reinvented across the world for over 5,000 years. Big jumps: Egyptian/Babylonian (~2000 BCE), Greek (~500 BCE), Indian (~500 CE — zero), Islamic (~800 CE — algebra), European (~1600 CE onward).

Marcus du Sautoy, The Story of Maths, Episode 1: The Language of the Universe · BBC / Open University, 2008 · ~60 min
Du Sautoy is an Oxford math professor. Episode 1 covers Egypt, Babylonia, and Greece.

Math is the closest thing humans have ever built to a universal language. If you meet an alien tomorrow, "2 + 2 = 4" would probably be the first thing you could agree on. Every civilization that got big enough figured out some form of math, and they all figured out the same things — because math is discovered, not invented.

But who figured out what and when matters a lot for what school actually teaches you. Here is the real map.

The Egyptians and Babylonians (~2000 BCE)

The first serious math we have written down comes from Egypt and Mesopotamia, roughly 4,000 years ago. Egyptians could do fractions, solve for unknowns, calculate areas of triangles and circles (their value for pi was ~3.16, close to the real 3.14159...).

The Babylonians had an even more sophisticated math. They used a base-60 number system — which is why we still divide hours into 60 minutes, minutes into 60 seconds, and a circle into 360 degrees. The Babylonians did this 4,000 years ago and we are still using it because 60 divides evenly by way more numbers than 10 does.

The Greeks (~500 BCE)

The Greeks turned math into proofs. Starting with Pythagoras and formalized by Euclid around 300 BCE, they asked: why is this always true? They developed the idea that you should prove things from a small number of starting axioms.

Euclid's Elements is one of the most influential books ever written. It stayed the standard geometry textbook for 2,000 years.

Archimedes essentially invented early calculus, worked out levers and buoyancy, and calculated pi to a much more accurate value. Eratosthenes measured the circumference of the Earth in 240 BCE, using shadows, to within 2% of the modern value.

The Greeks also had a big blind spot: they didn't really have a concept of zero as a number. This would be solved somewhere else.

India (~500 CE — the invention of zero)

Zero is probably the single most important mathematical invention nobody thinks about.

For most of human history, math had a problem: how do you write "no apples"? Turning zero into a real number you can add, subtract, multiply, and divide with — that was Indian mathematicians, sometime around 500 CE.

The Indian mathematician Brahmagupta (628 CE) wrote the first known rules for arithmetic with zero and negative numbers. Try to imagine doing math without those. You can't.

Indian mathematicians also invented the number system we still use — 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 0 — much better than Roman numerals for actually doing calculation.

The Islamic Golden Age (~800 CE — algebra)

Muslim mathematicians took the Indian number system, translated it into Arabic, and passed it westward to Europe. That is why we still call them Arabic numerals.

Around 820 CE, an Uzbek mathematician in Baghdad named Al-Khwarizmi wrote a book. The short version of its Arabic title was al-jabr — "reunion of broken parts." Al-jabr became algebra.

Al-Khwarizmi's name, translated into Latin as Algoritmi, gave us the word algorithm. Every time you say "algorithm" — about Google, about TikTok, about anything a computer does — you are naming a 9th-century Muslim mathematician. See our Arabia page.

Then Europe caught up (~1600 CE onward)

  • Descartes invented analytical geometry — combining algebra and geometry (the "x-y plane").
  • Newton and Leibniz, independently, in the 1660s–1670s, invented calculus — the math of change and motion — which is what makes all modern physics possible.
  • Euler, in the 1700s, wrote so much math that people still argue about how much of modern math is actually his.

Every one of these was building on 4,000 years of math done everywhere else in the world.

What humanity kept

Math is the closest thing we have to a shared human language. It works the same in every country. It travels across languages and religions and centuries better than anything else humans make.

Every piece of technology you use — every phone, every car, every plane, every computer, every video game, every microwave — runs on math. Every scientific fact we know is expressed in math. Every piece of engineering that stands up runs on math.

If you learn algebra in school, you are studying a 9th-century Muslim mathematician. If you use zero, you are using an Indian invention. If you write a number, you are writing an adapted Indian symbol. If you do geometry, you are using Greek rules. If you tell time, you are counting in Babylonian base-60.

Nobody owns math. Everyone helped build it.
