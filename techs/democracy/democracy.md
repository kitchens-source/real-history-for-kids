# Democracy

**Athens ~508 BCE (first written system) · Haudenosaunee ~1142 CE (the version that inspired the US Constitution) · still being fought over everywhere, right now**

School books usually say "democracy was invented in Ancient Greece." That's part of the story. But the version of democracy that most closely shaped **your** government — the U.S. Constitution — actually came from a different place, and mostly gets left out.

Both stories matter. Here they are, together.

## Athens (508 BCE)

An Athenian named **Cleisthenes** reorganized his city so that any adult male citizen could show up at an assembly, speak, and vote directly on laws. Officials were chosen by lottery from the citizen body. Juries too. This was radical. The Greeks called it *dēmokratia* — from *demos* ("the people") and *kratos* ("rule"). Rule by the people.

**The asterisks** (see our [Greece page](../../civs/greece/) for the whole story):

- "Citizens" meant free adult **Athenian men**. About 10–15% of the population.
- Women couldn't vote. Slaves — about a third of Athens — couldn't vote. Foreigners couldn't vote.
- Athens was also an empire that taxed and crushed cities that tried to leave its "alliance."

Still: this was **the first time in recorded history** that a large group of ordinary people ran their own government. Every democracy since owes something to Athens.

## The Haudenosaunee Great Law of Peace (~1142 CE, still in force today)

Around the year **1142 CE**, in what is now upstate New York, five Native American nations (a sixth joined later) stopped fighting each other and formed a union governed by a written constitution called the **Great Law of Peace**. See our [Haudenosaunee page](../../civs/haudenosaunee/) for the full story.

Some things the Great Law said, **hundreds of years before European countries figured them out**:

- Leaders serve the people, not the reverse. A bad leader can be removed.
- **Women picked the chiefs.** The clan mothers had the power to install and remove leaders.
- Every decision had to consider the impact **seven generations** into the future.
- All six nations had to agree — not majority rule.

In the 1740s, a Haudenosaunee leader named **Canassatego** told a group of British colonists: "You are weak because you fight each other. Do what we did — form a union." **Benjamin Franklin was in the room.** He wrote it down. He referenced it for the next 40 years.

When the American colonies designed the U.S. Constitution in the 1780s, Franklin openly cited the Haudenosaunee model. In **1988**, the U.S. Congress passed a resolution officially recognizing the Iroquois Confederacy's influence on the Constitution.

## What the American Founders took, and what they didn't

**Took:** Federal union of separate states. A two-chamber council (Senate and House). The idea that leaders serve at the people's consent. The idea that a bad leader could be removed (impeachment).

**Didn't take:**

- **Women voting.** That took another 150 years (1920, the 19th Amendment).
- **Everyone voting.** Black Americans got the legal vote in 1870 but the real vote in 1965 (Voting Rights Act). Native Americans didn't get citizenship until 1924. Poll taxes and literacy tests kept people out for even longer.
- **Seven-generation thinking.** Dropped completely.
- **Everyone must agree.** Dropped, replaced with majority rules.

## What humanity kept

The word "democracy" itself, and the whole disputed idea. Most countries today claim to be democratic, though what counts as "democratic" is constantly being fought over.

The version we have — representative democracy with regular elections, courts, and a written constitution — is a mash-up of Greek ideas, Haudenosaunee ideas, Roman republican ideas (see our [Rome page](../../civs/rome/)), medieval English legal traditions (Magna Carta 1215, Parliament), Enlightenment philosophy (John Locke, Montesquieu), and each democracy's own local history.

**Democracy is not one thing.** It has been reinvented, in different shapes, dozens of times. It has failed dozens of times too. Every generation has to defend it or lose it.

## The honest part

Athenian democracy died about 200 years after Cleisthenes. The Roman Republic died. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy was almost destroyed by disease and forced assimilation (it survived — see our [Haudenosaunee page](../../civs/haudenosaunee/)). Democracies fail when powerful people decide the rules don't apply to them and are allowed to get away with it.

That is not ancient history. That is right now, in every country that has a democracy, including yours.
