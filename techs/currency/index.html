Currency

First stamped coins: Lydia (modern Turkey), ~630 BCE · Paper money: China, ~900 CE · Cowrie shells as money: 3,000+ years across many societies

Niall Ferguson, The Ascent of Money, Episode 1: Dreams of Avarice · Channel 4 / PBS, 2008 · ~55 min
Official author's upload — traces money from Mesopotamian clay tablets through Lydian coins through modern banking

Money is one of those things you use every day without ever thinking about it. But someone had to invent it — and the whole idea of "here is a piece of metal or paper that means value, trust me" is one of the strangest and most important inventions humans have ever come up with.

Before money

For most of human history, people traded by barter — I give you a chicken, you give me a bag of grain. This works, but only up to a point.

The problems: what if I have a chicken and you have grain but you don't want a chicken? What if you have grain and I want grain but I don't have anything you want? What if my chicken is worth more than your grain but you don't have a way to give me "half a bag"?

Different societies came up with different solutions:

  • Cowrie shells — small pretty shells from the Indian Ocean. Portable, durable, hard to counterfeit. Used as money in China, India, Africa, and parts of North America for over 3,000 years. One of the longest-lived forms of money in human history.
  • Cattle — the word "capital" comes from Latin capita meaning "heads" (of cattle). So does "pecuniary" (from pecu).
  • Grain, salt, cloth, cocoa beans — Roman soldiers were sometimes paid in salt, which is where the word "salary" comes from.

But all of these have problems: cattle are hard to move, grain rots, cloth wears out. What people wanted was something valuable, portable, durable, and standardized.

The invention of coins

Around 630 BCE, in the kingdom of Lydia (in what is now western Turkey), someone had the idea: take a small lump of precious metal, stamp it with the king's official seal, and guarantee its weight and purity by that stamp. If people trusted the king, they would trust the coin.

The first Lydian coins were made of electrum, a natural mix of gold and silver from the Pactolus River. (This is the origin of the story of King Midas — the Lydian kings really were fantastically rich from that river.)

Before coins, every merchant had to carry scales, weights, and touchstones to check metal offered in trade. Coins made trade about ten times faster. You could just count them. The Lydians got rich. Their idea spread.

The other inventors of money

  • China was casting standardized bronze coins around the same time as Lydia (~600 BCE), independently. Chinese coins had a square hole in the middle so you could string them on a rope — a design that lasted 2,500 years.
  • India was minting silver coins by around 500 BCE.
  • China invented paper money around 900 CE — roughly 700 years before Europe. (See our China page.)
  • The Yap people of Micronesia used giant stone wheels — some 12 feet across, weighing thousands of pounds — as money. Nobody actually moved the stones. They agreed "this stone belongs to that person now." Which is worth thinking about: money has always been mostly a shared story people agree to believe.

What humanity kept

Every economy on Earth today runs on money in some form. The idea that a piece of paper, a stamped metal disc, or (now) a number in a computer can represent value that everyone agrees to accept — that is a Lydian invention, or a Chinese invention, or a Yap invention, depending on which shape you want to trace.

Money changed everything about how people live. It let cities grow beyond what farming villages could support. It let empires collect taxes from millions of people. It made specialization possible — you can be a full-time potter or scribe or musician because you can sell what you make for money and buy food.

It also made new kinds of injustice possible. Rich people can hoard money forever. Debts can be inherited. People without money can starve while food sits unsold. Every economy since Lydia has been trying to figure out how to keep the good parts of money without letting the bad parts get out of control.

The honest part

Money is not real in the way rocks and trees are real. It is real because we all agree it is real. Currency is one of the most powerful shared beliefs humans have ever created.

That means it can also collapse when the shared belief goes. Look up the Weimar Republic hyperinflation (1920s Germany) or Zimbabwe (2000s). In both cases, wheelbarrows of paper money couldn't buy a loaf of bread.

Money works because we agree it does. It is worth understanding that.
