# Currency

**First stamped coins: Lydia (modern Turkey), around 630 BCE. Paper money: China, around 900 CE. Cowrie shells as money: hundreds of societies, thousands of years.**

Money is one of those things you use every day without ever thinking about it. But someone had to invent it — and the whole idea of "here is a piece of metal or paper that means value, trust me" is one of the strangest and most important inventions humans have ever come up with.

## Before money

For most of human history, people traded by **barter** — I give you a chicken, you give me a bag of grain. This works, but only up to a point.

The problems: what if I have a chicken and you have grain but you don't want a chicken? What if you have grain and I want grain but I don't have anything you want? What if my chicken is worth more than your grain but you don't have a way to give me "half a bag"?

Different societies came up with different solutions:

- **Cowrie shells** — small pretty shells from the Indian Ocean. Portable, durable, hard to counterfeit. Used as money in China, India, Africa, and parts of North America for over **3,000 years**. Some African societies used them until the 1900s. They are one of the longest-lived forms of money in human history.
- **Cattle** — in many old societies. The word "capital" comes from Latin *capita* meaning "heads" (of cattle). So does "pecuniary" (from *pecu*, cattle).
- **Grain, salt, cloth, cocoa beans** — all used as commodity money in different places. Roman soldiers were sometimes paid in salt, which is where the word "salary" comes from.

But all of these have problems: cattle are hard to move, grain rots, cloth wears out. What people wanted was something that was **valuable, portable, durable, and standardized**.

## The invention of coins

Around **630 BCE**, in the kingdom of **Lydia** (in what is now western Turkey), someone had the idea: take a small lump of precious metal, stamp it with the king's official seal, and guarantee its weight and purity by that stamp. If people trusted the king, they would trust the coin.

The first Lydian coins were made of **electrum**, a natural mix of gold and silver that came from the Pactolus River nearby. (This is the origin of the story of King Midas, who supposedly turned everything he touched to gold — the Lydian kings really were fantastically rich from that river.)

Before coins, every merchant had to carry scales and weights and touchstones to check whether the metal offered in a trade was real and how much it weighed. **Coins made trade about ten times faster.** You could just count them. The Lydians got rich. Their idea spread.

## The other inventors of money

Coins were not the only kind of money invented, and Lydia was not the only place inventing money.

- **China** was casting standardized bronze coins around the same time as Lydia (~600 BCE), independently. Chinese coins had a square hole in the middle so you could string them on a rope, which turned out to be a great design that lasted 2,500 years.
- **India** was minting silver coins by around 500 BCE.
- **China invented paper money** around 900 CE, roughly 700 years before Europe. Bank drafts, then government-issued banknotes. This was so much more convenient than lugging around bags of coins that the Chinese economy became one of the biggest in the world. (See our [China page](../../civs/china/) for the whole story.)
- **The Yap people** of Micronesia used **giant stone wheels** — some of them 12 feet across, weighing thousands of pounds — as money. Nobody actually moved the stones. They agreed that "this stone belongs to that person now" and that was enough. Which is worth thinking about: **money has always been mostly a shared story people agree to believe.**

## What humanity kept

Every economy on Earth today runs on money in some form. The idea that a piece of paper, a stamped metal disc, or (now) a number in a computer can represent value that everyone agrees to accept — that is a Lydian invention, or a Chinese invention, or a Yap invention, depending on which shape you want to trace.

**Money changed everything about how people live.** It let cities grow beyond what farming villages could support (because you can trade with strangers, not just people you know). It let empires collect taxes from millions of people (try imagining collecting taxes in chickens). It let strangers do business with each other. It made specialization possible — you can be a full-time potter or scribe or musician because you can sell what you make for money and buy food.

It also made new kinds of injustice possible. Rich people can hoard money forever. Debts can be inherited. People without money can starve while food sits unsold. Every economy since Lydia has been trying to figure out how to keep the good parts of money without letting the bad parts get out of control.

## The honest part

Money is not real in the way rocks and trees are real. It is real because we all agree it is real. Currency is one of the most powerful shared beliefs humans have ever created.

That means it can also collapse when the shared belief goes. Look up the Weimar Republic hyperinflation (1920s Germany) or Zimbabwe (2000s) if you want to see what happens when people stop believing in a currency. In both cases, wheelbarrows of paper money couldn't buy a loaf of bread.

Money works because we agree it does. It is worth understanding that.

## If this grabbed you

- 🎥 **Watch:** [Niall Ferguson, *The Ascent of Money*, Episode 1: Dreams of Avarice](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2XX2b2MeDe0) (Channel 4 / PBS, 2008). Ferguson is a historian; this is the official author's upload. About 55 minutes. Traces money from Mesopotamian clay tablets through Lydian coins through modern banking.
- 📚 **Read:** *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* by David Graeber — the big scholarly history that overturns a lot of textbook stories about money.
- 📚 **Read:** *A History of the World in 100 Objects* by Neil MacGregor — has excellent chapters on the first coins and cowrie shells.
- 📚 **Read for kids:** *If You Made a Million* by David M. Schwartz — a picture book that teaches kids what money actually is.
- 🌍 **Visit online:** [The British Museum's Money collection](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/search?keyword=coin) — one of the world's biggest collections of historical coins.
