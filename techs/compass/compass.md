# The Compass

**Invented in China ~200 BCE for feng shui and divination. Adapted for maritime navigation in China around 1040 CE. Reached Europe about 100 years later. Rewrote what humans could do with the ocean.**

Before the compass, you could only sail by looking at the stars, the sun, the coastline, and by knowing your local waters. Cross a big ocean out of sight of land, on a cloudy night? You would be lost and probably die. So people mostly stayed close to shore.

Then someone in China noticed something about magnetized needles.

## The Chinese invention (~200 BCE, then ~1040 CE)

Chinese scholars had known for a long time that **lodestone** — a naturally magnetic mineral — pointed in a consistent direction when suspended freely. Around the **Han dynasty (~200 BCE)**, they were using this for **feng shui** — the art of arranging buildings and objects in harmony with the natural directions. Early Chinese compasses were shaped like ceremonial spoons that would spin around and point south (Chinese compasses traditionally pointed south, not north — same magnet, different convention).

For over 1,000 years, the compass was mostly a scholarly and religious tool, not a navigation instrument.

Then, around **1040 CE**, during the Song dynasty (see our [China page](../../civs/china/)), a scholar and government official named **Shen Kuo** wrote about a new use for compasses: **navigation**. He also noticed and wrote down **magnetic declination** — the fact that a compass points to magnetic north, not true north, and the difference varies depending on where you are on Earth. He figured this out in the **11th century**. Europeans did not notice it for another 400 years.

By the Southern Song (~1100s CE), Chinese sailors were routinely using floating-needle compasses on ships in the South China Sea and the Indian Ocean. The compass **transformed** long-distance ocean trade. Chinese ships were suddenly the biggest, fastest, and most far-ranging in the world.

## The compass reaches Europe (~1150 CE)

The knowledge traveled west along the Silk Road and by Arab-Muslim traders working the Indian Ocean. Europeans first wrote about the compass around **1180 CE**. It probably came in through Muslim Spain and Sicily, both of which were multi-cultural crossroads at the time.

For Europeans, the compass was a big deal. It meant you could sail out of sight of land safely. Combined with better ships, this eventually enabled:

- **The Portuguese exploration of West Africa** (1400s CE)
- **Columbus's crossing to the Americas** (1492)
- **Vasco da Gama reaching India by sea** (1498)
- **Magellan's expedition circumnavigating the globe** (1519–1522)

## What humanity kept

Almost every device that knows which way is which — every GPS in every phone, every map app, every airplane, every ship, every rover on Mars — has a magnetic compass function at its core, sometimes as a physical component and sometimes just as a concept.

The compass is one of the most world-changing inventions in history — arguably more world-changing than the wheel, because the wheel didn't rearrange whole continents.

## The complicated part

The same technology that let Europeans reach places they had never been also let them **enslave, conquer, and colonize** those places. Without the compass, the trans-Atlantic slave trade, the destruction of Native American civilizations by disease and force, the European colonization of Africa and Asia — none of it happens the way it did.

That is not the compass's fault. But it is honest to say: the compass didn't come with instructions for how to use it responsibly, and humans mostly didn't.

Every powerful technology has this shape. The compass was the first one to have it at this scale. It would not be the last.
