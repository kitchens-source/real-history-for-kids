The Compass

Invented in China ~200 BCE for feng shui · Adapted for maritime navigation ~1040 CE · Reached Europe ~1180 CE

Video for this page: not yet found. This library only embeds videos from directly verified real institutions. Michael Wood's Story of China Episode 3 (The Golden Age) covers the Song dynasty compass — search the title on YouTube. Reading list below is verified.

Before the compass, you could only sail by looking at the stars, the sun, the coastline, and by knowing your local waters. Cross a big ocean out of sight of land, on a cloudy night? You would be lost and probably die. So people mostly stayed close to shore.

Then someone in China noticed something about magnetized needles.

The Chinese invention (~200 BCE, then ~1040 CE)

Chinese scholars had known for a long time that lodestone — a naturally magnetic mineral — pointed in a consistent direction when suspended freely. Around the Han dynasty (~200 BCE), they were using this for feng shui. Early Chinese compasses were shaped like ceremonial spoons that would spin around and point south (Chinese compasses traditionally pointed south, not north — same magnet, different convention).

For over 1,000 years, the compass was mostly a scholarly and religious tool.

Then, around 1040 CE, during the Song dynasty (see our China page), a scholar named Shen Kuo wrote about a new use: navigation. He also noticed and wrote down magnetic declination — the fact that a compass points to magnetic north, not true north, and the difference varies depending on where you are. He figured this out in the 11th century. Europeans did not notice for another 400 years.

By the Southern Song (~1100s), Chinese sailors were routinely using floating-needle compasses. Chinese ships were suddenly the biggest, fastest, and most far-ranging in the world.

The compass reaches Europe (~1150 CE)

The knowledge traveled west along the Silk Road and by Arab-Muslim traders. Europeans first wrote about the compass around 1180 CE. Combined with better ships, this eventually enabled:

  • Portuguese exploration of West Africa (1400s CE)
  • Columbus's crossing to the Americas (1492)
  • Vasco da Gama reaching India by sea (1498)
  • Magellan's expedition circumnavigating the globe (1519–1522)

What humanity kept

Almost every device that knows which way is which — every GPS in every phone, every map app, every airplane, every ship, every rover on Mars — has a magnetic compass function at its core.

The compass is one of the most world-changing inventions in history — arguably more world-changing than the wheel, because the wheel didn't rearrange whole continents.

The complicated part

The same technology that let Europeans reach places they had never been also let them enslave, conquer, and colonize those places. Without the compass, the trans-Atlantic slave trade, the destruction of Native American civilizations by disease and force, the European colonization of Africa and Asia — none of it happens the way it did.

That is not the compass's fault. But it is honest to say: the compass didn't come with instructions for how to use it responsibly, and humans mostly didn't.

Every powerful technology has this shape. The compass was the first one to have it at this scale. It would not be the last.
