# The Printing Press

**Movable type invented in China around 1040 CE by Bi Sheng. Metal movable type perfected in Korea in 1377 CE. Johannes Gutenberg's press in Germany around 1440 CE was the version that took over Europe and changed everything.**

If you had told a European in 1450 that in one lifetime, books would go from being **rare, hand-copied treasures that only rich people and monks could touch** to being everyday objects that ordinary families owned — they would not have believed you. But that is exactly what happened, and the reason was one man, in one workshop, in one small German town.

Except — that story is only half true. Let's tell the whole story.

## The forgotten inventors

The idea of using movable little blocks with letters on them (**movable type**) so you can rearrange them to print any page you want was first invented in **China around 1040 CE** by a man named **Bi Sheng**. His blocks were made of baked clay. It worked. But Chinese writing has **thousands** of different characters (one per word, not 26 letters like English), so you would need thousands of different blocks to print anything, which was a huge amount of work. The idea stayed a niche technology.

Then, in **Korea in 1377 CE** — 63 years before Gutenberg — Korean craftsmen invented **metal movable type** and printed a Buddhist book called the **Jikji**. It is now in a library in Paris. It is the oldest surviving book in the world printed with metal movable type. Almost nobody in the West has heard of it.

## What Gutenberg actually did

Johannes Gutenberg was a goldsmith in the German town of Mainz. Around 1440, he combined a bunch of existing pieces into a system that had never quite been put together before:

- **Metal type letters** cast from a mold (like Korea), but designed for the small European alphabet, which meant a printer only needed about 300 different pieces to print anything.
- **Oil-based ink** thick enough to stick to metal type (his big personal invention — regular water-based ink for stamps did not work).
- **A screw press**, borrowed from the design used to press grapes for wine and olives for oil, adapted to press paper onto inked type.
- **Paper**, which Europe had recently learned to make in bulk (paper was invented in China around 100 CE, took over a thousand years to reach Europe).

None of those pieces was new. Gutenberg's genius was putting them all together into a **system** where one worker could print in one day what used to take a scribe an entire year to copy.

His first big project was a Bible. It took him three years and nearly bankrupted him. He printed about 180 copies. About 49 of those Bibles still survive today, and each one is worth more than a house.

## What happened next was completely wild

Before Gutenberg, there were maybe a few thousand books in all of Europe, and most of them were in monasteries. Fifty years after Gutenberg, there were **twenty million** printed books in Europe. Fifty years after that: **two hundred million**.

Books got cheap. Ordinary people started learning to read because now there was something to read. Then the wild stuff started:

- **The Protestant Reformation (1517).** Martin Luther nailed a list of complaints about the Catholic Church to a church door. Before Gutenberg, that would have been a local argument. After Gutenberg, his complaints were printed and spread across Europe in a month. Christianity split in half. That never gets undone.
- **Scientific Revolution.** Scientists could now read each other's work across countries. Copernicus, Galileo, Newton — they were all reading books that had been printed and shipped to them. Science stopped being a bunch of separate people guessing and started being a big shared conversation.
- **The idea of "the public."** Before printing, "what people think" mostly meant "what your neighbors think." After printing, newspapers and pamphlets meant that millions of people could form opinions about the same events at the same time. Modern politics — good and bad — was born.
- **Governments freaked out.** Every European monarch tried to control what got printed, because they knew that whoever controlled printing controlled what people believed. Some things never change.

## What humanity kept

Every book you have ever read is a descendant of Gutenberg's Bible. Every newspaper, every magazine, every printed poster, every business card. The internet is what happens when you take Gutenberg's core insight — "cheap copying of information for anyone" — and make it a billion times faster and free.

You are, right now, reading this because someone in China made paper 2,000 years ago, someone in Korea cast metal letters 650 years ago, and a German goldsmith combined those pieces 585 years ago into a machine that made ideas cheap.
