Writing

First invented in Mesopotamia (modern Iraq) around 3200 BCE · Invented independently at least three more times

Irving Finkel, How to Write Cuneiform · The British Museum, Curator's Corner S4 · ~12 min
The British Museum's cuneiform curator teaches you to actually write it, using a popsicle stick

Writing is so ordinary you never think about it. But someone had to invent it — and the person who did was doing bookkeeping.

The very first writing

Around 3200 BCE, in the city of Uruk in Mesopotamia, someone got tired of trying to remember whose sheep were whose. So they took a piece of wet clay and pressed marks into it with the end of a cut reed — one mark for one sheep, and a symbol next to it for whose sheep. They let the clay dry. Now the record was permanent.

That was it. That was writing.

The system they invented is called cuneiform (say it: "kyoo-NEE-uh-form"). It means "wedge-shaped," because the reed made little wedges in the clay. Cuneiform is the oldest writing system in the world, and it was used for over 3,000 years. The people who invented it were called the Sumerians.

The oldest writing we have is not poetry or holy books or history. It is receipts.

"Kushim signed for 29,086 measures of barley, delivered over 37 months."

That is the oldest signed document on Earth. Kushim — a bookkeeper — is the first person in human history whose name we still know.

Writing was invented more than once

This is the part that blows people's minds. Writing was so useful that different groups of humans invented it from scratch, independently, without ever meeting each other:

  • Sumerians in Mesopotamia — around 3200 BCE (cuneiform)
  • Egyptians — around 3200 BCE (hieroglyphs). Might have gotten the idea from Sumer, or might have invented it separately. Historians argue.
  • Chinese — around 1200 BCE (oracle bone script, ancestor of modern Chinese)
  • Mesoamericans (Olmec, then Maya) — around 900 BCE. Definitely invented completely on their own. Nobody in Central America could have talked to anybody in Sumer.

Four separate groups of humans, on different continents, in different centuries, all independently figured out: "if I make a mark that stands for a word, other people can read what I said even when I am not there."

Why writing changed everything

Before writing, if you knew something, it lived in your head. When you died, it died. Any knowledge that lasted more than one lifetime had to be memorized by younger people, word for word, and passed down that way. This is called "oral tradition," and human beings are actually really good at it — but you lose things.

After writing:

  • A person could know something a stranger learned a thousand years ago and a thousand miles away.
  • Contracts could be enforced because you had proof of what someone agreed to.
  • Governments could keep track of huge numbers of people and things — which meant they could get much bigger.
  • Ideas could travel further than any person ever could.

You are, right now, using something invented in 3200 BCE by an Uruk accountant to receive thoughts from a stranger you have never met. That is not normal. That took a very long time to become possible.

What humanity kept

Every written language on Earth today descends from one of those four independent inventions. The English alphabet you are reading traces back through Latin → Greek → Phoenician → an ancient Egyptian shorthand → Egyptian hieroglyphs. Your letters are, no exaggeration, 5,000-year-old modified drawings.
