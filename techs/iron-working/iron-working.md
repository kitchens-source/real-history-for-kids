# Iron Working

**Figured out around 1500–1200 BCE, in Anatolia (modern Turkey) by the Hittites. Kept secret. Then the secret got out, and the world changed.**

Before iron, the best metal humans had for tools and weapons was **bronze** — a mix of copper and tin. Bronze is soft, and both the copper and the tin had to come from far away (they are not usually found in the same place), so bronze was expensive and only rich people had good bronze weapons. Poor soldiers fought with sharpened sticks.

Iron is different. Iron is **everywhere**. It is one of the most common metals in the planet's crust. But there is a problem: to turn raw iron ore (which looks like a rock) into usable iron, you have to heat it to almost **1,500 degrees Celsius** (2,700 degrees Fahrenheit). That is really hot. So hot that ordinary wood fires cannot do it.

## The Hittite secret

The **Hittites** were a people who lived in what is now Turkey. Around 1500 BCE, they figured out a way. They made a special charcoal fire using bellows (leather pumps) to blow air into it, which made it burn much hotter than a normal fire. They also figured out that you have to add just the right amount of carbon (from the charcoal) to the iron while it is melting, or the iron comes out either too brittle or too soft.

**They kept the recipe secret for about 300 years.** Iron weapons gave Hittite armies a huge advantage: iron is harder than bronze, holds an edge better, and — most importantly — you could make a lot of it, because iron ore was cheap and everywhere.

Then, around **1200 BCE**, the whole ancient world fell apart. Historians still argue about why (drought, earthquakes, invasions, all at once). But when the dust settled, the Hittite Empire was gone, and their metalworkers had scattered across the Mediterranean. The secret was out.

## What happened when the secret got out

Iron went from being rare and magical to being ordinary. And "ordinary" is the interesting part.

- **Poor soldiers could now afford iron weapons.** Armies got bigger and cheaper. Wars got more dangerous for everybody.
- **Farmers could afford iron plows.** An iron plow can cut through hard soil that a bronze or wooden plow cannot. This meant much more land could be farmed. Places that had been forest for thousands of years got turned into fields.
- **Iron tools meant more food.** More food meant more people. The population of the ancient world grew significantly after 1000 BCE, and iron is a big part of the reason.
- **Blacksmiths became one of the most important jobs in every village.** For the next 3,000 years — until factories replaced them — every town had a blacksmith. Many still do.

## What humanity kept

The Iron Age never really ended. **You are still in it.** The metal in your car, in the beams holding up your house, in the frying pan in your kitchen, in the nails everywhere — it is iron (or steel, which is iron with a little bit of carbon deliberately mixed in, the exact trick the Hittites figured out).

The industrial revolution in the 1700s and 1800s was largely a story about learning to make **enormous** amounts of iron and steel — big enough to build bridges, ships, railroads, and skyscrapers. Every one of those uses the same basic chemistry the Hittites worked out over 3,000 years ago: heat the ore very hot, add just the right amount of carbon, hammer or roll it into shape.

## What humanity paid for it

Iron is also why, starting around 1200 BCE, wars started killing people at rates the world had never seen. When only rich soldiers had good weapons, wars were small and quick. When any farmer could be handed an iron spear, wars got much, much bigger. Every advance in human technology since then has had this shape: the more powerful it makes us, the more damage we can do with it. That is worth thinking about.

- 🎥 **Watch:** *The Hittites* (2003, narrated by Jeremy Irons). A full documentary about the empire that invented iron working. About 90 minutes. Available on several streaming and documentary sites — search the title.
- 📚 **Read:** *Ancient Iron Production* by Vincent C. Pigott — the technical version, for older kids who want to know exactly how the chemistry works.
- 📚 **Read for kids:** *A Boy Called Bat* is not about iron, sorry, this is a real gap. For iron age content pitched at kids, the DK *Ancient Civilizations* series has a Hittites section, and the Horrible Histories YouTube channel has short sketches.
- 🌍 **Visit online:** [The Metropolitan Museum's collection of Hittite objects](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/hd/anat/hd_anat.htm) — including some of the earliest iron artifacts humans have.
