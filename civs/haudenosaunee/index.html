The Haudenosaunee

The Iroquois Confederacy · founded ~1142 CE · still exists today

Chief Oren Lyons, The Roots of American Democracy · Bioneers, 2014 · ~20 min
Faithkeeper of the Turtle Clan of the Onondaga Nation

Long before there was a United States, there was already a democracy running in what is now upstate New York. Five nations — the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca (the Tuscarora joined later, making six) — stopped fighting each other and formed a union. They called themselves the Haudenosaunee, which means "People of the Longhouse." The name Europeans gave them, "Iroquois," is what most old history books still use.

The Great Law of Peace

The union was held together by a constitution called the Great Law of Peace — an actual written-down set of rules for how the six nations would share power, settle arguments, and go to war (or not). It was memorized and passed down using wampum belts, patterns of purple and white shell beads that worked like a physical record.

Some of the things the Great Law said, hundreds of years before European countries figured them out:

  • The leaders serve the people, not the other way around. A leader who stopped listening could be removed.
  • Women picked the leaders. The clan mothers — the older women of each family — chose the chiefs and could unseat them if they went bad. In Europe at the same time, most women couldn't own property.
  • Every decision had to think seven generations ahead. Not "what's best for me?" but "what's best for the great-great-great-great-grandchildren of the people who aren't born yet?"
  • All six nations had to agree. Not majority rules. Everyone. That forced people to actually talk until they found something everyone could live with.

What humanity kept from the Haudenosaunee

Here is the part American schools usually skip.

In the 1740s, a Haudenosaunee leader named Canassatego told a group of British colonists: you people are weak because you keep fighting each other. You should do what we did — form a union. He told them to imagine a bundle of arrows: one arrow snaps easily, but a bundle is strong.

Benjamin Franklin was in the room. He wrote it down. He kept writing about it for years. When the American colonies were designing their own government in the 1770s and 1780s, Franklin openly said the Haudenosaunee model was one they should study.

The United States Constitution ended up with a lot of Haudenosaunee ideas in it: a federal union of separate states, a two-chamber council (the House and Senate), the idea that leaders serve the people. In 1988, the U.S. Congress officially passed a resolution recognizing that the Iroquois Confederacy influenced the Constitution.

But the U.S. left out the other parts:

  • Women did not get to vote for another 150 years after the Constitution was written.
  • The "seven generations" rule got dropped completely.
  • The "everyone has to agree" rule got dropped, replaced with majority rules.

What happened to the Haudenosaunee

The short honest version: their land was taken, their people were killed by diseases they had no immunity to, their kids were sent to boarding schools designed to erase their language and culture. This happened on purpose, with laws passed by the same U.S. government that borrowed their political ideas.

They are still here. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy still exists. It still has chiefs, clan mothers, and a Grand Council that meets at Onondaga. They still issue their own passports (which they use to travel internationally to lacrosse tournaments — lacrosse is their game, they invented it). The Great Law of Peace is still in force. That is not "history." That is right now.
