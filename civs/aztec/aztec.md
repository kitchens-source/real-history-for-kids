# The Mexica (Aztecs)

**Central Mexico · Tenochtitlán founded 1325 CE · Spanish conquest 1521 CE**

The people you probably learned about as "the Aztecs" called themselves the **Mexica** (say it: "meh-SHEE-kah"). That is the name they used for themselves. "Aztec" was a name European writers came up with hundreds of years later. Both words show up in books today, and this page will use "Mexica" first because that is what they answered to.

The Mexica get taught two ways in most American schools, and both are wrong. One version says they were bloodthirsty savages who tore hearts out of chests and deserved to be conquered. The other says they were peaceful and were the innocent victims of European monsters. Real history is neither one, and the real story is much more interesting than either.

## The city that took your breath away

In 1519, when the Spanish soldier Bernal Díaz first saw the Mexica capital **Tenochtitlán** (say it: "teh-nawch-teet-LAHN"), he wrote:

> "When we saw so many cities and villages built in the water and other great towns on dry land we were amazed and said that it was like the enchantments they tell of. And some of our soldiers even asked whether the things that we saw were not a dream."

Bernal Díaz was not a poet. He was a professional killer who had already been in dozens of battles across the Caribbean. And he wrote that.

Here is what he was looking at:

- **A city of about 200,000 people** — bigger than any city in Spain at the time. Bigger than London. About the same size as Paris.
- **Built on an island in the middle of a lake.** Three long causeways (raised roads) connected it to the mainland.
- **Floating gardens** called **chinampas** — rectangles of packed lake mud and reeds, anchored to the lake bottom, that grew the food for the whole city. Chinampas produce **seven crops a year**. Modern farms mostly get one.
- **Aqueducts** that brought fresh drinking water from the mountains into the city.
- **Public latrines** and a system of night-soil collection (imagine a garbage truck for pee and poop, going through the city every night to keep it clean). London in 1519 had none of this. London had raw sewage in the streets for another 300 years.
- **A picture-writing system** (glyphs), astronomy, mathematics, a calendar more accurate than the European calendar in use at the time.
- **A market** where 60,000 people traded every day. Bernal Díaz said it was bigger than the market in Rome.
- **Free universal schooling for boys and girls.** Not "free public school for some." Every kid attended. Girls learned different things than boys — this was not a modern-style equal school system — but the idea of educating every child was not something Europe did for another 400 years.

The Mexica built this in **200 years**. Two hundred. From nothing.

## The uncomfortable part

Yes, they did human sacrifice. This is real. It was religious — they believed the sun would stop rising without it — and it was more common than in most societies of the time. Nobody serious denies this.

But **conquistador propaganda inflated the numbers by 10x or more** to justify the conquest. Modern archaeologists estimate the actual annual number of sacrifices in the whole empire was in the hundreds to low thousands. That is a lot. It is also fewer people than European countries were executing for religious "crimes" (heresy, witchcraft) in the same century. Spain, France, and England were burning people at the stake in public by the tens of thousands during the same period — and they did not stop because of ethics, they stopped much later.

The honest way to say it: **the Mexica did brutal things. So did every big empire in that era, including the ones that conquered them.** No civilization gets a free pass, including this one.

## What actually killed the Mexica

Movies show Spanish soldiers with guns defeating Mexica warriors with spears. That is not really what happened.

- **Smallpox.** The Spanish (and the rats and pigs they brought) carried diseases the Americas had never seen. **Nine out of every ten Native Americans died** from these diseases — probably tens of millions of people across both continents. The city of Tenochtitlán lost most of its population to smallpox in a single year. When the Spanish finally attacked, they were fighting a city already destroyed by illness.
- **Alliances with other Native peoples.** The Mexica had a lot of enemies — cities and peoples they had conquered and mistreated. When Cortés arrived, tens of thousands of Native warriors from the **Tlaxcalan** and other groups joined him because they wanted revenge on the Mexica. The Spanish were a small part of the actual fighting force.
- **Then the Spanish took credit.** After the conquest, Spanish writers scrubbed the Tlaxcalans and disease out of the story and told it as "a few brave Spaniards beat a mighty empire." That version stuck.

## What humanity kept from the Mexica

**One:** Chocolate. Cacao was Mexica (and earlier Maya). They drank it as a bitter spiced drink. Europe took it back home, added sugar, and now it is everywhere.

**Two:** Tomatoes, chili peppers, corn, squash, beans, avocado, vanilla, peanuts, pumpkins, papaya. Look at the food you eat this week. Somewhere between a third and a half of it started as Mexica (and other Mesoamerican) agriculture.

**Three:** Mexico. There are still 1.5 million people who speak **Nahuatl**, the Mexica language, today. The name "Mexico" comes from "Mexica." When you order a Mexican meal, you are eating the direct legacy of the people who built Tenochtitlán.

**Four:** The chinampa farming system. Modern researchers studying sustainable agriculture are re-learning how the Mexica did it because it is one of the most productive farming techniques ever invented — and does not need fossil fuels or synthetic fertilizer.
