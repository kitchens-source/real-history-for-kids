# Ancient Greece

**Mediterranean · roughly 800 BCE to 146 BCE (when Rome absorbed it)**

Almost everything Americans are taught about ancient Greece is a story of one city — **Athens** — during about one hundred years — roughly 500 to 400 BCE. That's real, and it's important. But it's like teaching "American history" as "New York City, 1920 to 2020." You'd miss a lot.

This page covers three things: what Athens actually was (spoiler: harder than it sounds), what the rest of Greece was doing at the same time, and what the Greeks actually contributed to humanity that is still with you today.

## Athens: the birthplace of democracy, with some very big asterisks

Around **508 BCE**, an Athenian named **Cleisthenes** reorganized his city's government so that ordinary male citizens — not just noble families — could vote on laws directly, in a big assembly. Any adult male citizen could show up and speak. Laws passed by majority vote. Jurors were picked randomly from the citizen body.

This was a huge deal. It was the first known system where a large group of ordinary people ran their own government. Almost every democracy since traces some inspiration back to Athens.

**The asterisks:**

- **"Citizens" meant free adult Athenian men.** That was about **10% to 15%** of Athens's actual population.
- **Women could not vote or hold office.** Ever. Not even the wives of the most powerful men.
- **Slaves could not vote or hold office.** And slaves were about **a third of the population**. Athens was a slave society — a real, industrial-scale one, with slaves working in silver mines under brutal conditions.
- **Foreigners could not vote.** Even if your grandfather had lived in Athens, you were still not a citizen.
- **Athens was an empire.** After winning against Persia (see our [Persia page](../../civs/persia/) for the other side of that story), Athens ran the "Delian League" — a bunch of other Greek cities that were supposed to be allies, but that Athens taxed heavily and crushed if they tried to leave.

Athenian democracy is a big deal because it was **the first** — not because it was perfect. Real democracy for everyone took over 2,000 more years to start showing up, and in many places is still being worked on.

## The rest of Greece

Ancient Greece was **not one country**. It was hundreds of independent city-states (called **poleis**) speaking the same language, sharing gods and stories, and often fighting each other.

- **Sparta** — Athens's biggest rival. Not a democracy but a militarized oligarchy where boys were taken from their families at seven for brutal military training. Sparta ran on enslaved conquered people called **helots**, who outnumbered actual Spartans about 10 to 1. Spartan women had more rights and property than women almost anywhere else in the ancient world, which sometimes gets forgotten.
- **Thebes** — briefly the strongest city in Greece in the 300s BCE, home to the "Sacred Band" of Thebes, an elite unit made up of 150 pairs of male lovers who fought as couples. They defeated Sparta and were considered nearly unbeatable for decades.
- **Miletus, Ephesus, and the Ionian cities** on what is now the coast of Turkey — this is actually where a lot of Greek philosophy and science started, not in Athens. Thales, Anaximander, Heraclitus, Hippocrates — all from Ionian cities, not Athens.
- **Syracuse** in Sicily — a huge, rich Greek city that most Americans have never heard of. Home to Archimedes, the greatest mathematician of the ancient world.

## What humanity kept from Greece

**Philosophy.** The idea that you could think carefully about big questions — "what is real?", "what is right?", "how should we live?" — using reason, without needing a priest or a king to tell you the answer. Every Western philosopher since Plato and Aristotle has been in conversation with those two, whether they know it or not.

**Democracy** (with all the asterisks above). Also the words we use for it: democracy, politics, tyrant, monarch, oligarchy. All Greek.

**Science and mathematics.** Euclid's geometry is still what your textbook teaches. Archimedes worked out how buoyancy and levers work. Eratosthenes measured the circumference of the Earth in 240 BCE to within 2% of the modern value, using shadows. The Greeks did not invent science, but they made it a formal discipline.

**The Olympics.** Started in Olympia around 776 BCE. Kept going for almost 1,200 years. Restarted in 1896 as the modern Olympics.

**Theater.** Comedy, tragedy, actors on a stage, all Greek inventions. Every play, movie, and TV show you have ever watched descends from what the Greeks worked out.

**The whole idea of "the West."** Which is a slippery, sometimes misleading category, but real: the tradition that says Europe and its cultural offshoots (America, Australia, etc.) share something called "Western civilization" that traces back through Rome to Greece. This is partly true, partly overstated (much of Greek knowledge came from Egypt, Persia, and Mesopotamia originally, then was preserved through the Islamic world during Europe's dark ages — see our [Arabia page](../arabia/)). But the story is real, and Greece is a big part of it.

## The honest, complicated part

If you meet someone who tells you "ancient Greece is where civilization started" — they are telling you the Western textbook version, which is wrong. **Egypt, Mesopotamia, China, and India were all older, larger, and often more advanced than Greece in specific ways.** What Greece did was uniquely take a bunch of ideas from all those places and reshape them into something that would later be picked up by Rome, then by Europe, then by the Americas.

Also: ancient Greek society had a version of slavery that was industrial-scale, tolerated pedophilia (specifically, sexual relationships between adult men and adolescent boys — this was actually considered normal in Athens), and treated women as property. When someone romanticizes "the classical world," they are usually skipping these parts.

Greeks did amazing things. So did lots of other people. And Greeks also did some very bad things. All of that is real.
