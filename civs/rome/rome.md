# Rome

**Central Italy, then Mediterranean, then most of Europe · 753 BCE (traditional founding) to 476 CE (Western Rome falls) · Eastern Roman Empire lasted until 1453 CE**

Rome is one of those subjects your school books cover a lot but usually flatten into two vibes: **gladiators + Caesar + toga parties** (fun history) or **the greatest civilization ever built** (Western-flattery history). Both miss what actually made Rome interesting.

Here is what Rome actually was: **a small farming town that spent 500 years figuring out how to run a big country, and then spent another 500 years running one, and then spent another 1,000 years trying to hold it together after it started falling apart.** That is a real long time. Most of what you experience about how governments work today comes from Rome trying and failing and trying again.

## The Roman Republic (509–27 BCE)

For its first 500 years, Rome had **no king**. This is important, and most Americans don't get taught it.

The Romans **hated** kings. They had kicked out their last king around 509 BCE and set up a system called the **Republic** where power was shared. Two elected officials called **consuls** ran things for one year each, then went back to being ordinary citizens. A senate advised them. Different assemblies represented different classes of citizens.

The word "republic" comes from Latin *res publica*, which means "public thing" or "public affair." That's the idea: the government belongs to the public, not to one guy.

**When the American Founders were designing the U.S. government in the 1780s, they read Roman history obsessively.** The word "senator" is Roman. The word "capitol" comes from Rome's Capitoline Hill. The eagle on American money is a Roman military symbol. The Founders wanted to build a Roman-style republic and were terrified of Rome's cautionary tale — what happens when a republic dies.

## The cautionary tale: Julius Caesar

After 500 years of the Republic, Rome had gotten too big. Generals had private armies. Rich Romans owned so much land that ordinary farmers were being pushed out. The political system stopped working because everyone was fighting to get an edge.

Enter **Julius Caesar**, a brilliant general who conquered Gaul (roughly modern France), came home richer than anyone, and eventually made himself dictator-for-life. The Senate murdered him in **44 BCE** to save the Republic — but it did not work. Caesar's adopted son **Octavian** finished the job and made himself the first Roman Emperor. His new name: **Augustus**.

The Republic was over. Rome would be run by emperors — some good, most bad — for the next 500 years.

The American Founders read this story as a warning: **republics die when powerful men are allowed to break the rules for long enough.** They tried to build safeguards. Whether those safeguards work is still an open question in every generation.

## The Roman Empire (27 BCE – 476 CE)

Under the emperors, Rome kept expanding until it ruled from Scotland to Iraq, from Morocco to Ukraine — about **75 million people**, roughly a quarter of all humans alive on Earth at the time.

Some things the Roman Empire actually did:

- **Roads.** Over 250,000 miles of engineered roads across the empire, built so well that some are still in use 2,000 years later.
- **Aqueducts.** Fresh water piped from mountain springs into cities. Rome itself got about 300 million gallons a day. Modern London did not match that until the 1900s.
- **Concrete.** Roman concrete was actually **better** than modern concrete for some uses — it gets **stronger** in seawater, which we still cannot fully replicate.
- **Sanitation.** Public toilets, sewers, public baths where ordinary people could wash. Then all of that was forgotten for over 1,000 years after Rome fell.
- **Standard weights, measures, currency, and law.** A merchant could travel from London to Damascus and use the same money, the same measurements, and expect the same legal protections.
- **Citizenship.** By 212 CE, every free adult male in the empire was a Roman citizen with legal rights. That is more people than had legal rights again anywhere until the 1800s.
- **Multi-ethnic empire that mostly worked.** Roman emperors were African (Septimius Severus), Syrian (Elagabalus), Iberian (Trajan). Rome didn't care where you came from if you were loyal.

## The honest, complicated part

- **Slavery.** At its peak, Rome had **more slaves than the American South ever did** — millions of them, doing everything from farming to teaching kids to serving in wealthy households. Roman slavery was often less racialized than American slavery (a slave could theoretically buy their freedom or become an important person's assistant and gain status) but it was still forced labor at enormous scale.
- **Gladiators.** Yes, real. Also mostly slaves, and yes people died for entertainment in the Colosseum, and yes hundreds of thousands of people watched. Modern professional sports do not, on average, kill their participants for the crowd. Rome did.
- **Conquest was brutal.** When Rome conquered a place that resisted, entire cities got wiped out. When Rome destroyed Carthage in 146 BCE, they killed or enslaved essentially every inhabitant. When Rome crushed a rebellion in Judea in 70 CE, they destroyed Jerusalem and started the diaspora that shaped Jewish history for the next 2,000 years.
- **The Republic died because rich Romans corrupted it.** That's the boring, non-glamorous version, and it's the real one. If you want the story of what killed Rome, start there.

## What humanity kept from Rome

**The whole idea of "law."** Roman law is the direct ancestor of most modern legal systems, including American law. Concepts like "innocent until proven guilty," contracts, property rights, wills — Roman.

**Latin.** Which is not spoken anymore but became French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, and Romanian, and gave English about half its vocabulary.

**Christianity.** Started as a small Jewish religious movement in Roman-occupied Judea, became the state religion of the Roman Empire in 380 CE, and from there spread everywhere Rome went (and eventually everywhere Europe went, which is most of the world).

**The idea of "the West."** Rome carried Greek ideas forward, added Roman law and administration, and passed the whole package to medieval Europe. Every European country that came later was, in some way, trying to be a smaller Rome.

**The template of what a republic is and how it can die.** Every generation of Americans has to re-learn this. So does everyone else.
