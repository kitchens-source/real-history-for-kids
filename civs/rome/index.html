Rome

Central Italy → Mediterranean → most of Europe · 753 BCE (traditional founding) to 476 CE (Western Rome falls) · Eastern Roman Empire to 1453 CE

Mary Beard, Ultimate Rome: Empire Without Limit, Episode 1 · BBC, 2016 · ~60 min
Mary Beard, Cambridge Roman historian, narrates and walks you through Rome

Rome is one of those subjects your school books cover a lot but usually flatten into two vibes: gladiators + Caesar + toga parties or the greatest civilization ever built. Both miss what actually made Rome interesting.

Here is what Rome actually was: a small farming town that spent 500 years figuring out how to run a big country, and then spent another 500 years running one, and then spent another 1,000 years trying to hold it together after it started falling apart. Most of what you experience about how governments work today comes from Rome trying and failing and trying again.

The Roman Republic (509–27 BCE)

For its first 500 years, Rome had no king. This is important, and most Americans don't get taught it.

The Romans hated kings. They had kicked out their last king around 509 BCE and set up a system called the Republic where power was shared. Two elected officials called consuls ran things for one year each, then went back to being ordinary citizens.

The word "republic" comes from Latin res publica, which means "public thing." The government belongs to the public, not to one guy.

When the American Founders were designing the U.S. government in the 1780s, they read Roman history obsessively. The word "senator" is Roman. The word "capitol" comes from Rome's Capitoline Hill. The eagle on American money is a Roman military symbol. The Founders wanted to build a Roman-style republic and were terrified of Rome's cautionary tale — what happens when a republic dies.

The cautionary tale: Julius Caesar

After 500 years of the Republic, Rome had gotten too big. Generals had private armies. Rich Romans owned so much land that ordinary farmers were being pushed out. The political system stopped working because everyone was fighting to get an edge.

Enter Julius Caesar, a brilliant general who conquered Gaul (roughly modern France), came home richer than anyone, and eventually made himself dictator-for-life. The Senate murdered him in 44 BCE to save the Republic — but it did not work. Caesar's adopted son Octavian finished the job and made himself the first Roman Emperor. His new name: Augustus.

The Republic was over. Rome would be run by emperors — some good, most bad — for the next 500 years.

The American Founders read this story as a warning: republics die when powerful men are allowed to break the rules for long enough. They tried to build safeguards. Whether those safeguards work is still an open question in every generation.

The Roman Empire (27 BCE – 476 CE)

Under the emperors, Rome kept expanding until it ruled from Scotland to Iraq, from Morocco to Ukraine — about 75 million people, roughly a quarter of all humans alive on Earth at the time.

  • Roads. Over 250,000 miles of engineered roads, built so well that some are still in use 2,000 years later.
  • Aqueducts. Fresh water piped from mountains into cities. Rome got about 300 million gallons a day. London did not match that until the 1900s.
  • Concrete. Roman concrete was actually better than modern concrete for some uses — it gets stronger in seawater, which we still cannot fully replicate.
  • Sanitation. Public toilets, sewers, public baths. Then all forgotten for over 1,000 years after Rome fell.
  • Standard weights, measures, currency, and law. A merchant could travel from London to Damascus and use the same money and expect the same legal protections.
  • Citizenship. By 212 CE, every free adult male in the empire was a Roman citizen with legal rights.
  • Multi-ethnic empire that mostly worked. Roman emperors were African (Septimius Severus), Syrian (Elagabalus), Iberian (Trajan). Rome didn't care where you came from if you were loyal.

The honest, complicated part

  • Slavery. At its peak, Rome had more slaves than the American South ever did — millions of them, doing everything from farming to teaching kids. Roman slavery was often less racialized than American slavery but was still forced labor at enormous scale.
  • Gladiators. Yes, real. Mostly slaves. People died for entertainment in the Colosseum and hundreds of thousands watched.
  • Conquest was brutal. When Rome destroyed Carthage in 146 BCE, they killed or enslaved essentially every inhabitant. When Rome crushed a rebellion in Judea in 70 CE, they destroyed Jerusalem and started the diaspora that shaped Jewish history for the next 2,000 years.
  • The Republic died because rich Romans corrupted it. That's the boring, non-glamorous version, and it's the real one.

What humanity kept from Rome

The whole idea of "law." Roman law is the direct ancestor of most modern legal systems, including American law. Concepts like "innocent until proven guilty," contracts, property rights, wills — Roman.

Latin. Not spoken anymore but became French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, and Romanian, and gave English about half its vocabulary.

Christianity. Started as a small Jewish religious movement in Roman-occupied Judea, became the state religion of the Roman Empire in 380 CE, and from there spread everywhere Rome went.

The template of what a republic is and how it can die. Every generation of Americans has to re-learn this. So does everyone else.
