# India: The Mauryan Empire and Ashoka the Great

**South Asia · Mauryan Empire ~322 BCE to ~185 BCE · Ashoka's reign 268–232 BCE**

Most Americans learn about ancient Greece and Rome. Not many learn that during the same centuries, in India, there was an empire **bigger than either of them**, ruled by a man who did something no other emperor in history did before or after. His name was **Ashoka** (say it: "ah-SHOW-kuh"), and he might be the most interesting person you have never heard of.

## Before Ashoka

Ashoka's grandfather **Chandragupta Maurya** founded the empire around 322 BCE by uniting most of what is now India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh. It was one of the biggest empires the world had ever seen. When Chandragupta died, his son ran it. Then it passed to Chandragupta's grandson: Ashoka.

Ashoka was a great warrior and an even better killer. He crushed his brothers to become emperor. Then he expanded the empire even further. By his early middle age, he ruled around **50 million people** — one out of every three humans alive on Earth at the time.

## The war that changed everything

In **261 BCE**, Ashoka invaded a place called **Kalinga**, on the east coast of India. Kalinga was independent and had been resisting Mauryan control for decades. Ashoka decided to end it.

The battle was a total Mauryan victory. It was also one of the bloodiest battles of the ancient world. In Ashoka's own words, carved into stone later:

> "One hundred and fifty thousand people were carried away as captives. One hundred thousand were killed. And many times that number died."

The next morning, Ashoka walked across the battlefield.

**And something in him broke.**

He wrote about it. He said he saw the burned villages and the dead — soldiers and old people and children and animals — and he could not believe he had done this. He said the sound of the crying afterward was worse than the sound of the battle. He said the smell of the burning would not leave him.

## What no other emperor in history has ever done

Ashoka went home, and he **stopped**.

He did not stop being emperor. He kept ruling for the next thirty years. But he stopped conquering. He converted to **Buddhism** — a religion that was less than 300 years old at the time and preached non-violence. And then he did something completely unprecedented for a ruler of an empire that big:

**He wrote what he had learned onto giant stone pillars and rock faces**, spread all across his empire, so ordinary people could read them for the next 2,000 years.

Some of the things carved on Ashoka's pillars:

- All people, whatever their religion, deserve respect.
- Prisoners should be treated humanely.
- Roads should have shade trees and wells so travelers do not suffer.
- Hospitals should be built — for people **and** for animals.
- The strong must not hurt the weak.
- Never insult another person's religion, even if you think yours is right.
- Regret is real, and rulers should feel it publicly when they do wrong.

Many of the pillars are **still standing.** You can go to India and read Ashoka's own words in the stone, carved by his order 2,300 years ago. The lion capital from one of his pillars is on the modern Indian flag and every Indian government document today. India took its national symbol from Ashoka's promise of non-violence and tolerance.

## What humanity kept from Ashoka

**One:** The idea that a leader can publicly say "I was wrong, I did something terrible, I will not do it again" and that this makes them **stronger**, not weaker. Every leader who has ever admitted a mistake in public — from Abraham Lincoln to Nelson Mandela — is walking a path Ashoka opened first.

**Two:** The idea that the government should build things for people who cannot pay — hospitals, wells, shade for travelers, care for animals. Public services. Ashoka did this in 250 BCE.

**Three:** The idea that religious tolerance is not weakness but strategy. Ashoka's empire had Buddhists, Hindus, Jains, and older religions all living side by side, on purpose, by law. Compare to how European kingdoms would treat religious difference 2,000 years later.

**Four:** Buddhism itself spreading beyond India. Ashoka sent Buddhist missionaries to Sri Lanka, Central Asia, and possibly as far as Egypt and Greece. Without Ashoka, Buddhism might have stayed a small Indian religion. Because of Ashoka, it eventually became one of the world's largest — 500 million Buddhists today owe part of their tradition to him.

## The honest, complicated part

Ashoka did not undo the Kalinga War. The people he killed were still dead. His empire was built on his grandfather's conquests, which were not gentle. When Ashoka died, the empire started falling apart within 50 years — being a good ruler is not the same as building something that lasts.

But he tried. He tried harder than almost any powerful person has ever tried. And he wrote it down so we would remember.
