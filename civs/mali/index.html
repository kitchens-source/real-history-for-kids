The West African Empires

Ghana → Mali → Songhai · roughly 300 CE to 1591 CE

Gus Casely-Hayford, The powerful stories that shaped Africa · TEDGlobal 2017 · ~15 min
Open on TED.com if the embed doesn't play

Before Europeans knew Africa was there, one of the richest and best-run parts of the world was already running for over a thousand years in West Africa. Three empires took turns being in charge: Ghana, then Mali, then Songhai. Together they lasted longer than the United States has existed. At their peak, each of them stretched across land bigger than Western Europe.

What they got rich from

Two things people everywhere wanted: gold and salt.

Salt kept meat from spoiling before refrigerators existed, so it was worth almost as much as gold — in some places, they traded pound for pound. The gold mines were in the forests to the south. The salt mines were in the Sahara Desert to the north. West Africa sat right between them, and the traders had to come through. Each empire took a small cut of every deal that crossed their land.

That's how you build a great empire — not by conquering everyone, but by owning the road everyone else needs.

Mansa Musa: the trip that broke Egypt's economy

In 1324, the ruler of Mali went on a trip. His name was Mansa Musa, which means "King of Kings."

He wanted to visit Mecca, the holy city for Muslims, which was over 3,000 miles from his capital. He did not travel light.

He brought 60,000 people. He brought 12,000 servants, each carrying about four pounds of pure gold. He brought 80 camels, each carrying 300 pounds of gold dust. When he stopped in cities along the way, he gave gold to everyone he met — beggars, teachers, poets, kings.

He gave away so much gold in Cairo, Egypt that the value of gold there dropped for twelve years afterward. He accidentally crashed the economy of one of the biggest cities in the world just by being generous. No one before or since has ever moved that much wealth by being nice about it.

When he got home, he didn't spend the rest of his gold on palaces or armies. He spent it on books and universities.

Timbuktu: the city of libraries

Mansa Musa built the city of Timbuktu into one of the biggest centers of learning in the world.

The University of Sankore in Timbuktu had 25,000 students studying math, astronomy, medicine, law, poetry, and history. This was in the 1300s. At the same time, Oxford University in England had a few hundred students. Cambridge had fewer. The University of Paris was one of the biggest in Europe and had maybe 4,000.

Scholars from across Africa, the Middle East, and Europe came to Timbuktu to study. The city collected hundreds of thousands of handwritten manuscripts. Many of them are still being read today. Some were hidden by families for centuries — passed down parent to child through wars and invasions — just to keep them safe.

What humanity kept from West Africa

One: The Timbuktu manuscripts. Real evidence, written down by real people in real time, that some of the most sophisticated math, astronomy, and medicine of the medieval world was happening in West Africa. When someone tells you "history started in Europe," those manuscripts are one of the reasons that's just not true.

Two: Mansa Musa is still, adjusted for inflation, considered by many economists to be the richest person who has ever lived. Not Elon Musk. Not Jeff Bezos. A West African king from 700 years ago. When someone tells you Africa was poor before Europeans arrived, remember: Europeans came looking for what Mali already had, and they knew Mali was there because of Mansa Musa's trip.

Three: The empire didn't last forever. Songhai fell in 1591 when a Moroccan army with guns crossed the Sahara and defeated an army that had never fought against firearms. Empires end. That's part of the honest story too.
