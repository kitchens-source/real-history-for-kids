The Achaemenid Persian Empire

Middle East, Central Asia, and beyond · 550 BCE to 330 BCE

Neil MacGregor, 2600 years of history in one object · TEDGlobal 2011 · ~20 min
Open on TED.com if the embed doesn't play

Your school books probably tell you about Ancient Greece: the wars, the philosophers, the Olympics. They probably do not tell you that during the same centuries, right next door, there was an empire fifty times bigger than Greece running a completely different experiment in how to be a big country. That empire was Persia.

The Persians built the largest empire the world had ever seen. From modern Turkey to Egypt to India, from the Black Sea to the Persian Gulf, one government somehow held it all together for two hundred years.

Cyrus the Great and a new idea

In 539 BCE, the Persian king Cyrus conquered the city of Babylon — the biggest, richest city in the world at that time. This was normal for empires: you conquer a city, you kill the leaders, you enslave the people, you smash their gods, you make them worship yours.

Cyrus did something different.

He wrote what he was going to do on a clay barrel that is now called the Cyrus Cylinder (you can go see it at the British Museum). On it, he said: I am not smashing your temples. I am not making you worship my gods. If your people have been kidnapped by other empires, I am sending them home. If your temples are broken, I am helping you rebuild them.

One of the groups he sent home were the Jews, who had been forced from Jerusalem into Babylon fifty years earlier. That is why the Cyrus Cylinder is sometimes called the first document about human rights, and why the Bible calls Cyrus a hero.

How you run an empire that big

The Persians invented systems that everyone since has copied.

  • The Royal Road. A 1,600-mile paved highway from one end of the empire to the other. Messengers on horseback with fresh horses at every station could get a message across the whole empire in seven days. (Herodotus wrote about them: "Neither snow nor rain nor heat nor gloom of night stays these couriers from the swift completion of their appointed rounds." Sound familiar? That is the U.S. Postal Service motto. They stole it from the Persians.)
  • Satrapies. Instead of running the whole empire from one capital, they split it into 20 pieces called satrapies, each with a governor. Local languages stayed. Local religions stayed. As long as you paid your taxes and did not rebel, Persia mostly left you alone.
  • Standard weights and money. Before Persia, every city had its own weights and coins, which made trade a nightmare. Persia made one standard, and trade exploded.
  • Multiple languages, treated equally. Big announcements were carved in three languages side by side: Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian. Nobody's language was "the important one."

What humanity kept from Persia

One: The idea that you can rule people who are different from you without erasing them. Not by being nice — the Persians were not always nice, and they were an empire that took what it wanted — but by figuring out that tolerating differences is actually a stronger way to run things than crushing them.

Two: Postal systems, tax systems, standard weights and money, professional government administrators. If your grown-up gets mail, or pays taxes, or uses money that works the same in every store, some of that traces back to Persia.

Three: When the United Nations wrote the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in 1948, they hung a replica of the Cyrus Cylinder on the wall as a reminder that this is an old idea, not a new one.

Why you have not heard this before

Because most of what your school books know about ancient Persia was written by their enemies, the Greeks. Greeks and Persians fought big wars, and Greek writers like Herodotus made Persia sound scary and cruel so the Greek heroes looked better. That is normal — everyone talks trash about the team they are playing against. But it means we spent 2,500 years reading one side of the story.

The Persians wrote their own history too. Most of it is only being properly translated in the last hundred years or so, and it looks very different.
