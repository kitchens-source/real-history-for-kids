# The Fall of Tenochtitlán (1521)

**August 13, 1521 · The Spanish, with tens of thousands of Native allies, captured the Mexica capital after a three-month siege · This ended the Mexica Empire and began 300 years of Spanish colonial rule in Mexico**

You have read our [Mexica page](../../civs/aztec/). This is the specific event where their empire ended — and it did not happen the way most movies and school books tell it.

## The Hollywood version (mostly wrong)

Brave, brilliant Spanish conquistadors — a few hundred of them — armed with guns and steel and horses, defeated a mighty empire of millions of Aztecs using superior European tactics and technology. The Aztec emperor Montezuma foolishly welcomed Cortés thinking he was a returning god. Then Cortés cleverly conquered the empire.

Almost every part of this is wrong or radically incomplete.

## What actually happened

**April 1519.** A Spanish adventurer named **Hernán Cortés** landed on the coast of Mexico with about **500 soldiers and 16 horses**. He was there without official Spanish authorization — his boss, the governor of Cuba, had actually recalled him, but Cortés had gone anyway.

Cortés heard rumors of a rich empire inland. He decided to march there. First, though, he **burned his own ships** so his men couldn't turn back.

**On the way to Tenochtitlán, everything about the traditional story starts to break down.** Cortés was not conquering by his own strength. He was recruiting.

## The alliance that actually did the fighting

The Mexica had been the dominant power in central Mexico for about a century. They had gotten there by conquering and demanding tribute from every neighboring city. Many of those cities **hated the Mexica** and were waiting for a chance to strike back.

The most important were the **Tlaxcalans** — a people who had fought the Mexica for generations and never been fully conquered. When Cortés arrived, the Tlaxcalans first fought him (and beat him back several times), then realized his tiny Spanish force could be their useful weapon against Tenochtitlán. They allied with him.

**By the time Cortés reached Tenochtitlán in November 1519, his army was:**
- ~500 Spanish soldiers
- **Tens of thousands of Tlaxcalan warriors**, plus warriors from Cempoala, Huejotzingo, Chalco, and other cities that had grievances with the Mexica

That's not "a few hundred conquistadors." That's a huge Indigenous army with Spanish auxiliaries. It's really an anti-Mexica coalition that included some Spaniards.

## The first meeting with Montezuma

The Mexica emperor **Motecuhzoma II** (the English "Montezuma" is a corruption of his name) invited Cortés into Tenochtitlán and gave him and his men **imperial palaces to stay in**. He did **not** think Cortés was a god. That's a story invented later, mostly by Spanish writers trying to make the conquest sound divinely ordained. **Nothing in Mexica sources supports it.**

What Montezuma probably did think: this small group of strangers was interesting, potentially useful, potentially dangerous. He wanted to study them before deciding what to do. Meanwhile, his advisors were furious that he had let them into the city at all.

Cortés, feeling paranoid, **took Montezuma hostage inside his own palace**. Amazingly, this worked for a while. The Mexica court continued to run through Montezuma as a puppet emperor.

## The Noche Triste and the plague

In June 1520, while Cortés was away, one of his subordinates massacred a huge Mexica religious ceremony. The city exploded in rebellion. Montezuma was killed (Spanish sources say the Mexica stoned him; Mexica sources say the Spanish killed him — historians still argue).

Cortés and his men had to flee the city on the night of **June 30, 1520**, in what Spanish histories call the **Noche Triste** (Sad Night). Hundreds of Spaniards died. Cortés escaped with a fraction of his force.

**Then smallpox arrived.**

A single infected African crewman had come with the Spanish. Nobody in the Americas had ever seen this disease. The Mexica had no immunity. **In late 1520, smallpox raced through Tenochtitlán and killed an estimated 40% of the population in a few months** — including the new emperor Cuitláhuac, who had led the successful resistance during the Noche Triste.

**By the time Cortés came back with his rebuilt army in 1521, Tenochtitlán was a city already destroyed by disease.**

## The siege

Cortés returned in **May 1521** with about **900 Spaniards** and, more importantly, **80,000 to 200,000 Indigenous allies**. He also had the Tlaxcalans and others build a **fleet of small warships** he could put on Lake Texcoco to cut off Tenochtitlán by water.

The siege lasted **93 days**. The Mexica, weakened by disease and cut off from food and water, resisted street by street. The fighting was brutal and prolonged. When it ended on **August 13, 1521**, most of the city was destroyed. The last Mexica emperor, **Cuauhtémoc** (say it: "kwow-TEH-mok"), was captured while trying to escape by canoe. He was later executed.

**The Mexica Empire was over.** The Tlaxcalans and other allies who had helped Cortés discovered fairly quickly that they had traded one master for a much worse one. Spanish rule was harsher on them than Mexica rule had ever been. **Tlaxcala got some special protections early on, but most of the "allies" got the same colonial treatment as everyone else.**

## What made this possible

Not Spanish superiority. **Three things**, in order of importance:

**1. Smallpox and other diseases.** In the first century after 1492, Native American populations across both continents dropped by 90%. That is not a war casualty rate. That is the collapse of every society in the hemisphere from microbes they had no defense against.

**2. Native alliances against the Mexica.** The Mexica had made enemies of most of their neighbors. Those enemies did the majority of the fighting.

**3. Spanish weapons and horses, but only sort of.** Yes, steel weapons and horses gave individual Spanish soldiers an edge in some engagements. But without the smallpox and without the allies, 500 Spaniards would have been overrun.

## What humanity kept

**Mexico** itself. The name comes from *Mexica*. About 1.5 million people still speak **Nahuatl** — the Mexica language. The great pyramid platforms of Tenochtitlán are still under Mexico City, being excavated. The words *chocolate, tomato, avocado, coyote, chile* are all Nahuatl.

**The written record.** Amazingly, some Mexica scribes survived the conquest and, in the decades after, wrote down their own history in Nahuatl using the Latin alphabet the Spanish had taught them. These are called the **Florentine Codex** and other sources. **The Mexica told their own story of the conquest.** For centuries, European histories ignored these sources. In the last 50 years, historians like Camilla Townsend have brought them back into the mainstream. That's why what your grandparents learned about the Aztecs is different from what you're reading here.

## The honest, complicated part

The Spanish conquest of Mexico is not a story of Europeans defeating Native Americans. It is a story of **Native Americans doing most of the fighting on both sides**, in a war caused by a pandemic. Most of the Native peoples of central Mexico were victims of the conquest **twice**: first as the Mexica's tributary subjects, and then as Spain's colonial subjects.

The Spanish were not more moral than the Mexica or the Tlaxcalans. But they had luck (smallpox), timing (arriving right when internal grievances were peaking), and organization (a state that could send more soldiers, more priests, more administrators, year after year for centuries). That combination is what actually killed the Mexica Empire.
