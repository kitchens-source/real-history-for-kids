# The Mongol Sack of Baghdad (1258)

**February 1258 · Mongol armies under Hulagu Khan destroyed Baghdad, ended the Abbasid Caliphate, and effectively ended the Islamic Golden Age · Sometimes called the darkest week in the history of science**

Everything you read in our [Arabia page](../../civs/arabia/) — the House of Wisdom, algebra, algorithms, Ibn al-Haytham, Ibn Sina, five hundred years of Baghdad being the world's most sophisticated city — **it ended in one week in February 1258.**

## The setup

The **Mongols** were a nomadic people from the steppes of Central Asia. In the 1200s, under **Genghis Khan** (1162–1227), they exploded outward and conquered the largest contiguous land empire in human history — from Korea to Poland, from Siberia to Iran. Nothing had ever moved that fast.

Genghis Khan died in 1227. His empire was divided among his sons and grandsons. One of those grandsons was named **Hulagu Khan**. His territory was the western part — Persia and the Middle East.

Hulagu's mission from the Mongol Great Khan: **finish the conquest of the Islamic world.** Take Baghdad.

## Baghdad in 1258

By 1258, Baghdad was no longer at the peak of its power (the Golden Age had already started to fade in the 1100s), but it was still the largest city in the Islamic world, the seat of the **Caliph** (the religious head of Sunni Islam and legal successor to the Prophet Muhammad), and one of the most sophisticated cities on Earth. The **House of Wisdom** was still functioning. Its libraries contained the largest collection of books in the world — hundreds of thousands of manuscripts, some of them the last surviving copies of works from Greece, Persia, and India.

The Caliph in Baghdad in 1258 was named **Al-Musta'sim**. He was, unfortunately, incompetent. When Hulagu sent messengers demanding surrender and tribute, Al-Musta'sim refused, insulted the messengers, and claimed the Islamic world would rise to defend him. Nobody rose. His own advisors had been trying to warn him.

## What happened

Hulagu arrived at Baghdad in **January 1258** with an army estimated at **150,000 to 300,000 soldiers** — one of the largest Mongol armies ever assembled. He surrounded the city.

The siege lasted **12 days**.

On **February 10, 1258**, the walls fell. The Mongols poured in.

**What followed was, by most accounts, one of the worst massacres in human history.** The Mongols spent about a week systematically killing everyone they found. Estimates of the death toll range from **200,000 to over a million people**. Modern historians tend to think the real number was probably 200,000–800,000. Whatever the exact number, it was catastrophic.

Al-Musta'sim was captured. Because Mongol tradition forbade spilling royal blood on the ground, he was reportedly rolled up in a carpet and trampled to death by horses. He was the 37th and last Abbasid Caliph. The Caliphate that had ruled the Islamic world since 750 CE was over.

Then the Mongols burned the House of Wisdom.

**Contemporary chroniclers wrote that the Tigris River ran black with ink** for days, from the vast number of books thrown into the water. **Hundreds of thousands of unique manuscripts** — the accumulated scientific and philosophical work of the entire Islamic Golden Age, plus the earlier Greek, Persian, Indian, and Chinese texts they had translated — were lost forever.

We do not know exactly what was in those manuscripts. That is part of the tragedy: we do not know what we do not know. Every history of medieval science is written with a footnote saying "and much more was lost at Baghdad in 1258."

## What changed because of this

**1. The end of the Islamic Golden Age.** The Islamic world continued and eventually recovered, but the specific center of gravity — Baghdad as a place where science was happening — was over. Islamic science kept going in other places (Cairo, Cordoba, Samarkand, Isfahan) but never again at the concentration of the House of Wisdom.

**2. A permanent shift in the balance between civilizations.** For 500 years, the Islamic world had been ahead of Europe in almost every measurable way. After 1258, the gap started to narrow. By 1500, Europe had caught up. By 1800, Europe was ahead. Multiple factors contributed to this, but the destruction of Baghdad is one of them.

**3. The Mongols eventually converted to Islam.** Hulagu's descendants (called the Ilkhanate) eventually adopted Islam and became patrons of Islamic scholarship, which is one of history's stranger reversals. But by then, the damage was done.

## What humanity kept

- The Islamic scientific and philosophical tradition survived, just not concentrated in Baghdad. Cairo and Cordoba especially carried it forward.
- Some manuscripts survived — copies had been sent to other libraries in the centuries before the sack, and Baghdad's own books were still being read in translation across Muslim Spain, Sicily, and eventually Europe. Enough of the Islamic Golden Age's work made it out to reshape European learning during the Renaissance.
- **The Mongol Empire itself**, oddly, made global trade much safer along the Silk Road for about a century (the "Pax Mongolica"). This is how Marco Polo could travel from Venice to China and back safely. The same conquerors who destroyed Baghdad also connected the world in ways no one had before.

## The honest, complicated part

The Mongols get villainized in most Western and Muslim histories, and the sack of Baghdad is a big reason why. But the Mongol Empire also:

- Enforced religious tolerance across a huge territory — Genghis Khan's law code explicitly protected freedom of worship. Christians, Muslims, Buddhists, and shamans all coexisted under Mongol rule.
- Improved trade routes and communication across Eurasia.
- Made possible the exchange of technology (Chinese inventions reached Europe faster because of Mongol conquests) and disease (the Black Death also spread across Mongol trade routes, killing about a third of Europe in the 1300s).

None of that erases what happened at Baghdad. But **the Mongols were not one thing** any more than any other empire is. Real people. Real complexity. Real horror. Real influence. All at once.

## Something to think about

Whole libraries have been destroyed multiple times in human history — the Library of Alexandria (multiple times, over centuries), the Maya codex burnings by Spanish priests, Chinese Cultural Revolution book burnings, the Nazis, and many others. **The permanent loss of knowledge is one of the largest categories of tragedy in human history**, and it almost always happens because someone with power decided that a whole way of thinking should not exist.

**Every book that survives is because someone, somewhere, decided to protect it — often at real risk.** That's worth remembering.
