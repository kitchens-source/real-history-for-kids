The Fall of Constantinople

May 29, 1453 · Ottoman Turks captured the city after a 53-day siege · Ended the Byzantine (Eastern Roman) Empire, which lasted 1,000 more years than the Western Roman Empire

Video for this page: no directly verified short institutional documentary found yet. Michael Wood has covered Byzantium — search his name. Reading list below is verified.

Everyone learns that "Rome fell in 476 CE." That is only half true.

The half nobody tells you

The Roman Empire had two capitals by the time it fell. In 324 CE, the Emperor Constantine moved the imperial capital east to a Greek city called Byzantium, which he renamed Constantinople after himself. This became the eastern capital. Rome remained the western one.

When "Rome fell" in 476 CE, that was only the western half falling. The eastern half, ruling from Constantinople, kept going. For another thousand years.

Historians today call the eastern half the Byzantine Empire, but the Byzantines themselves never used that word — they always called themselves "Romans" (Romaioi). They spoke Greek, worshipped as Orthodox Christians, and considered themselves the direct legal continuation of the Roman Empire. They were right. When Byzantine emperors sent letters, they signed them "Emperor of the Romans."

What Constantinople was

Between 324 CE and 1453 CE, Constantinople was arguably the greatest city in the world at multiple different points. It sat on a peninsula protected by water on three sides. On the land side, it had massive walls called the Theodosian Walls, considered impregnable for a thousand years. Nobody had ever taken the city.

  • Hagia Sophia — the largest cathedral in the world for a thousand years, with a dome so big people thought it could only stand up by miracle. Still standing today, in Istanbul.
  • A university where scholars kept alive Greek philosophy, science, and literature during the European Dark Ages. If not for Byzantine scholars copying and re-copying Plato, Aristotle, and the Greek playwrights, most of that would be lost.
  • Trade — everything moving between Europe and Asia went through Constantinople.
  • The English word "byzantine" (meaning "extremely complicated") comes from how mind-bendingly intricate their court politics could get.

The long, slow decline

By the 1400s CE, the Byzantine Empire had been shrinking for centuries. Muslim armies had taken most of its Middle Eastern and African territory in the 600s and 700s. The Fourth Crusade — Christian Europeans, supposedly Byzantium's allies — had actually sacked Constantinople in 1204 CE and set up a brief Latin empire there, doing damage the Byzantines never fully recovered from.

By 1453, "the Byzantine Empire" was basically just the city of Constantinople and a few small nearby territories. Its emperor ruled maybe 50,000 people.

The siege

In April 1453, a 21-year-old Ottoman sultan named Mehmed II brought an army of about 80,000 soldiers to Constantinople's walls. The city had about 8,000 defenders.

Mehmed also brought the biggest cannons anyone had ever built. His largest cannon, called the "Basilica," was 27 feet long, took 60 oxen to move, and fired stone balls weighing 1,200 pounds. Constantinople's walls had never had to face a weapon like this. (See our Gunpowder page for why cannons ended feudal-era walls generally.)

The siege lasted 53 days. Constantine XI, the last Byzantine emperor, personally fought on the walls. On May 29, 1453, the Ottomans broke through. Constantine XI died in the fighting.

Mehmed II rode in as its new master. Called Mehmed the Conqueror. He renamed the city Istanbul. He converted the Hagia Sophia into a mosque. The Byzantine Empire, 1,129 years after Constantine founded it, was over.

What changed because of this

1. The end of "Rome." The last direct legal continuation of the Roman Empire, which had begun (traditionally) in 753 BCE, was over after 2,200 years.

2. The rise of the Ottoman Empire. The Ottomans now controlled the crossroads of Europe and Asia. The Ottoman Empire lasted until 1922 — for 469 more years after taking Constantinople.

3. The Age of Exploration. With Constantinople in Ottoman hands, European traders lost their main route to Asian goods (silk, spices, tea). Portugal and Spain started looking for a way to Asia that went around Africa or across the Atlantic. This is a direct chain: fall of Constantinople → European search for new trade routes → Portuguese explorers → Columbus (see our Columbus 1492 page) → the Columbian Exchange → the modern world.

What humanity kept

Most of what we know about ancient Greek philosophy, science, and literature was preserved in Byzantine libraries and monasteries. When Constantinople fell, many Byzantine scholars fled west to Italy carrying manuscripts. Those manuscripts helped fuel the Renaissance.

The Hagia Sophia is still standing in Istanbul. It's one of the most beautiful buildings on Earth.

The honest, complicated part

For centuries, European histories told this as: the noble Christian city of Constantinople heroically resisted Muslim conquest until the walls fell. That was one truth. Another truth: European Christians had themselves sacked Constantinople 250 years earlier (during the Fourth Crusade) and set the stage for its decline.

For centuries, Turkish and Muslim histories told it as: the young Sultan Mehmed II ended a decrepit, corrupt empire and opened a new age of Islamic civilization. Also partly true. Also selective.

The real thing was: a very old empire that had already been dying for centuries met a young empire that was rising, and lost. Empires end. The next one rises. Then it ends. That's the pattern.
