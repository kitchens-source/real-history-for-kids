# When Xerxes Tried to Conquer All of Greece (480–479 BCE)

**One of the most consequential 18 months in ancient history · If Persia had won, "Western civilization" as we know it does not exist**

You have probably seen the movie *300*. It is almost entirely wrong. It is also based on a real story that is much crazier than the movie version.

Here is what actually happened.

## The setup

The **Persian Empire** (see our [Persia page](../../civs/persia/)) was the largest empire the world had ever seen. Ruled from Iran, it stretched from Egypt to India, and it wanted to add **Greece** to that list.

In 490 BCE, the Persian king **Darius I** had already tried to invade Greece once and lost — spectacularly — at the **Battle of Marathon** (which is where the modern marathon race gets its name, from the runner who supposedly ran 26 miles to Athens to announce the victory and then dropped dead).

Darius died. His son **Xerxes** (say it: "ZUR-ksees") inherited both the empire and his father's grudge. He spent **four years** preparing to finish the job. He wanted **all of Greece**.

## The invasion

In the spring of 480 BCE, Xerxes crossed from Asia into Europe with the **largest army the ancient Mediterranean world had ever seen**. Ancient sources say a million soldiers — that's exaggerated. Modern historians estimate 100,000 to 300,000. Still, an army bigger than the population of most Greek cities put together.

To get his army across the Hellespont (the strait between Turkey and Europe), Xerxes had his engineers **build a bridge out of boats** — over 600 ships lashed together across almost a mile of open water. The bridge worked. His army walked across.

His fleet, over 1,000 warships, sailed south along the coast to support the army.

The Greek city-states, who normally spent all their time fighting each other, formed a temporary alliance and prepared to defend themselves. Most of the Greek cities were terrified. Some **surrendered without a fight**, sending Persia the traditional gifts of "earth and water" as symbols of submission. Athens and Sparta refused.

## Thermopylae: the 300 (with 6,700 friends)

The Greeks knew they could not stop the Persian army in open ground — the numbers were too lopsided. So they picked a place where the Persians couldn't use their numbers: a narrow mountain pass called **Thermopylae**, where the coast squeezes between the mountains and the sea. In some spots the pass is only 15 meters wide. Perfect for a small force to hold against a big one.

The Spartan king **Leonidas** brought his royal bodyguard — **300 hand-picked Spartan warriors** — along with **about 7,000 other Greek soldiers** from various cities. That's the part *300* the movie leaves out: **the 300 Spartans were not fighting alone**. There were nearly 7,000 Greek allies with them.

They held the pass for **three days** against the entire Persian army. Xerxes threw wave after wave of soldiers at them, including his elite "Immortals." Greek casualties were minimal. Persian casualties were staggering.

Then a Greek traitor named Ephialtes told the Persians about a **secret goat path** through the mountains that got around the pass. The Persians used it to attack the Greeks from behind.

Leonidas sent most of the Greek army home. He kept the 300 Spartans, plus **700 Thespians and 400 Thebans**, to hold the pass for one more day so the rest could escape. All of them were killed. This is the famous "Last Stand" — a real event, more or less like the legend, though the numbers of the last stand were closer to 1,400 than 300.

Then the Persian army marched south, burned Athens to the ground, and looked ready to conquer everything.

## Salamis: the naval battle that decided history

The Greek fleet — mostly Athenian, commanded by a general named **Themistocles** — retreated to the narrow **Strait of Salamis** (a little strip of water between an island and the Athenian coast).

Themistocles tricked Xerxes into attacking there. Xerxes had over 1,000 ships. The Greeks had 371. But the strait was so narrow that Xerxes couldn't use his numbers — his own ships got in each other's way. The Greek ships, smaller and more maneuverable, rammed the Persian ships one after another.

**The battle was catastrophic for Persia.** Xerxes lost hundreds of ships. He personally watched the disaster from a golden throne set up on a mountain overlooking the strait.

Xerxes decided he'd had enough. He left most of his army in Greece under a general named Mardonius and sailed home.

## Plataea: the finish

The next year (479 BCE), the Greeks — again in a rare unified force — met Mardonius's remaining army at **Plataea** and destroyed it. Mardonius was killed. The Persian invasion was over.

**Persia never seriously tried to conquer Greece again.**

## Why this matters, honestly

For centuries, Western historians told this story as: **freedom-loving Greek democracy defeated Persian tyranny**. Some of that is right. Athens really was a democracy (with all the asterisks — see our [Greece page](../../civs/greece/)). Persia really was ruled by a king with absolute power.

But it is worth remembering what Persia's alternative version of the world was: a **multi-ethnic empire that mostly tolerated local religions and cultures** and let conquered peoples keep their own languages (see our [Persia page](../../civs/persia/)). If Persia had won, Greeks would have been ruled from Persepolis, paying taxes to a Persian satrap. They would probably not have been enslaved en masse or forcibly converted. The Persian version of "getting conquered" was actually less brutal than what Rome would do a few centuries later.

Also: **the "300 heroic Spartans" myth is a very selective use of history.** Sparta itself was a slave society where a tiny warrior class dominated a massive population of enslaved conquered people called **helots**. When Americans and Europeans in the 1800s and 1900s glorified the "Spartan sacrifice at Thermopylae," they were often quietly using the story to celebrate their own militarism and their own racial hierarchies.

**Was Xerxes' defeat a good thing for humanity?** Historians actually still argue this. The most honest answer is: it changed the shape of history in ways nobody can rerun. Greek philosophy, science, and democracy went on to shape "the West." That would have looked very different (and possibly not existed) under Persian rule. Whether that would have been worse or better on balance — no one really knows.

## What humanity kept

**Marathon** (the race, the distance). **The word "laconic"** (from *Lakonia*, the Spartan region, because Spartans were famous for talking very little — Leonidas's alleged final speech before Thermopylae reportedly consisted of the word "MOLON LABE" — "come and take them" — when Xerxes demanded the Spartans surrender their weapons).

The whole tradition of **outnumbered underdogs standing against an empire and winning**. Every American Revolutionary War book, every Star Wars movie, every "we're outnumbered but we won't back down" story is a direct descendant of Thermopylae and Salamis.

And, indirectly: the world that made possible ancient Greek philosophy, drama, and democracy — the seeds of what would eventually become the ideas your school books talk about as "Western civilization." That world existed because Xerxes lost.
