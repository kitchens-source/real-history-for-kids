The Fall of the Western Roman Empire

September 4, 476 CE · A Germanic warlord named Odoacer deposed the last Roman emperor in the West · The eastern half (Byzantine) kept going another 977 years — see our Fall of Constantinople page

Video for this page: no directly verified short institutional documentary found yet. Reading list below is verified.

Everyone learns "Rome fell." What actually happened is much slower, weirder, and more interesting than that phrase suggests.

Rome did not fall in a day

By 476 CE, the "Roman Empire" in the west had been slowly dying for at least 200 years. Things that had been chipping away at it:

  • Massive plagues. The Antonine Plague (165–180 CE) and the Plague of Cyprian (249–262 CE) killed millions. Roman armies got smaller. Roman cities lost population. Tax revenue collapsed.
  • Climate change. The Roman warm period was ending. Growing seasons got shorter. Barbarian peoples on the frontier had less food and pushed south, into Roman territory.
  • Civil wars. In the 200s CE alone, Rome had 26 emperors in 50 years, most murdered by their own soldiers.
  • Christianity replaced the old civic religion. Not necessarily bad — the debate is huge — but it changed what people were willing to fight for.
  • The tax base collapsed. Rich Romans stopped paying taxes and moved to fortified rural estates. The army couldn't be paid.
  • Roman armies were now mostly non-Roman. By 400 CE, most Roman soldiers were Germanic recruits led by Germanic generals, loyal to their commanders, not to Rome as an idea.

What actually happened in 476

The Western Roman Empire had already been split, invaded, sacked (by the Visigoths in 410, the Vandals in 455), and reduced to essentially just Italy by the late 400s. Its "emperors" were increasingly powerless figureheads controlled by whichever Germanic general was in charge that year.

In 476 CE, a Germanic warlord named Odoacer — who had been fighting for Rome — decided he was tired of pretending. He deposed the last Western emperor, a teenage boy named Romulus Augustulus ("Little Augustus"). Odoacer sent Rome's imperial regalia to the eastern emperor in Constantinople as a way of saying "we don't need this in the west anymore." Then he ruled Italy himself as king.

Nobody at the time thought this was "the end of civilization." Life in Italy under Odoacer was very similar to life under the last Roman emperors. The Roman Senate kept meeting. Roman law kept being enforced. Latin kept being spoken. Christianity kept spreading. The eastern half — Byzantium — kept going and considered itself the real Rome.

The idea that 476 was "the fall of Rome" was mostly invented by later historians. The people alive at the time barely noticed.

What actually collapsed

  • Cities. Population of the city of Rome went from a million people at its peak to about 20,000 by 550 CE. Aqueducts stopped being maintained. Public baths closed. Public education ended.
  • Long-distance trade. Without imperial protection, moving goods hundreds of miles by land got dangerous.
  • Writing. Literacy in Western Europe crashed. Books were still copied — but only in monasteries, in Latin (which stopped being spoken and became a scholarly-only language).
  • Complex specialized labor. Most people had to become subsistence farmers again. Skilled trades — glassmaking, sophisticated metallurgy, indoor plumbing — got lost or scaled way down.

This is what people mean by "the Dark Ages" — a real drop in the material standard of living across most of Western Europe from about 500 CE to about 1000 CE. It was uneven, but it was real.

What replaced it

  • Germanic kingdoms carved out of the old empire — Franks in France, Angles and Saxons in England, Ostrogoths and Lombards in Italy, Visigoths in Spain.
  • The Byzantine Empire in the east — kept going, ruled from Constantinople, still calling itself Rome. Kept most of Roman law, Greek learning, and complex city life going for another thousand years.
  • The Catholic Church — filled the political and cultural vacuum in Western Europe. Monasteries became the schools, hospitals, libraries, and welfare offices of medieval Europe.
  • The Islamic world, starting in the 600s — quickly took over most of the old Eastern Roman territories in Egypt, Syria, and North Africa. Became the center of world science for 500 years (see our Arabia page).

What humanity kept

Everything, eventually. Roman law became the basis of most modern legal systems. Latin became French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Romanian, plus half of English. Roman architecture ideas came back in the Renaissance and are still used today.

The idea of "empire" itself — a large multi-ethnic state ruled by one central government — is a Roman template. Every empire since Rome has, in some way, tried to be Rome.

And, most importantly for our purposes: the idea of Rome, of a republic, of a citizen, of the rule of law, was preserved through the Dark Ages and rediscovered during the Renaissance and Enlightenment. When the American Founders sat down to design the U.S. Constitution in 1787, they were reading Cicero, Tacitus, and Livy. Rome fell in the west in 476. The idea of Rome did not.

The honest, complicated part

For over 1,500 years, "Rome fell" has been a political weapon. Different people have blamed different things: Christianity (Edward Gibbon, 1776), immigration (18th-century racial theorists), moral decay (every generation), bad economics (modern economists), climate change (modern historians), just too much bad luck (some modern historians).

The honest answer is: all of these, and more, over hundreds of years. No single cause. If someone tells you "Rome fell because of X," and X is their political enemy, be suspicious.

Also: Rome had already lasted 500 years as a Republic and another 500 as an Empire. Almost nothing lasts that long. The interesting question is not "why did Rome fall?" but "how did it last as long as it did?"
